C++ from scratch

Drone Design

From-scratch hardware and software implementation of a drone (quadcopter).

Motivation

Why I built this

For my grade 12 tech class, I had to spend an entire semester on a single project, and I thought it would be fun to build a drone. To push myself, I decided to design all the hardware and write all the software from scratch rather than buying an off-the-shelf flight controller. Below is a video of the drone flying — I never got around to implementing a stabilization algorithm, which is why it's visibly unstable in the air.

Approach

How I built it

The drone was built around 4 motors, each driven by its own electronic speed controller (ESC), all controlled by an ESP32 (WROOM) microcontroller acting as the brain of the drone. The ESCs were PWM-controlled and powered through a custom power distribution board that ran from a fuse to a lithium-ion battery. A gyroscopic sensor was also wired into the ESP32 for orientation data. For input, the drone connected wirelessly over Bluetooth to a PS3 controller, using an open-source library to handle the pairing and communication.

I wrote all the system control code in C++. Sadly, I've since lost the actual source for this project, but I still remember the basic structure. There was a main class for ESC control, with 4 objects, one per ESC. Another class handled gyroscope data and continuously computed roll and pitch. A third class handled controller input and output, including haptic feedback — I'd rumble the controller based on total motor power, roll, and pitch. A fourth class handled safety logic: if I walked too far away and the controller disconnected, or if the drone's angle changed too quickly, it would cut power to all motors.

Reflection

What I learned

One of the most important things this drone taught me was to think about safety features from day one, not as an afterthought. Originally, I had none: no power cutoff, no throttle acceleration limiting, and no cutoff for excessive tilt. Predictably, that caused problems, and the first couple of incidents taught me exactly why safety needs to be designed in from the beginning. For the record, no one was ever hurt and nothing outside the drone itself was damaged — but I did shatter a propeller against a locker, damage my microcontroller's pins, and dent the drone's frame.

After that, I spent a couple of days writing nothing but safety logic. That pass added a manual power shutoff button, a cutoff for excessive tilt, a cutoff for excessive tilt acceleration, throttle limiting, an automatic shutoff if the controller lost connection, a shutoff triggered by a sudden change in acceleration (i.e., the drone hitting something), and a shutoff if the ESCs or gyroscope lost connectivity. After adding those features, I didn't have another incident.
