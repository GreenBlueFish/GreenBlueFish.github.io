Motivation

My Story with Laptops, Distros, and Networking

There's a local computer store my family goes to that fixes computers and sells both new and second-hand machines. One day I went in and asked if they had any old laptops they couldn't sell, or that didn't work, so I could try to fix them up and play around with them. To my surprise, they said yes and handed me 3.5 Lenovo ThinkPad Yoga 11Es (the .5 being a spare screen). I took them back to my dorm room and spent a night fixing them up. They all had issues — one's screen wouldn't turn on, another beeped in odd patterns, and the third was missing both a battery and a hard drive. I got two of them working by swapping screens, batteries, hard drives, fans, and keyboards, and installing fresh operating systems on them. I remember starting with the Linux distro Debian, running a GUI. I ended up giving away one of the laptops since I didn't need both, and kept messing around with Debian on the other — poking through LibreOffice, playing a few games, and digging into partition tables.

A little while later I got curious about computer networking and found out about Cisco Packet Tracer. It was free, so I downloaded it and took a few courses. They covered the basics — how to wire up a network, and what a switch, router, server, and modem each did — then moved into more fundamental skills like what an IP address, MAC address, and subnet mask were. It also covered DHCP and the basic protocols for both DHCP and Ethernet. I learned about LAN, WAN, SOHO networks, and the seven layers of the OSI model. The courses also went into different network protocols like Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and NFC, and touched on SSIDs. I got hooked, and building simple networks in Cisco Packet Tracer let me put all of it into practice.

After a while, I wondered if I could get another computer and try to get them talking to each other over Ethernet, with no switch or router involved. I figured it would be easy — just set the port settings to manual DHCP and give each computer a unique IP address (192.168.0.x with subnet 255.255.255.0). I was too scared to try this on my personal laptop in case I broke something, so I went back to the store and, to my surprise, walked out with 2 more laptops (a Lenovo T520 and a Lenovo L440). Both worked, but they were old and slow, still running Windows 8. I installed Linux Mint Cinnamon on the L440 and Kali Linux on the T520. I then tried connecting the two computers directly via their Ethernet ports, but couldn't get it working — I later found out it's not possible on older machines, since they don't automatically swap TX and RX. So I just left them lying around. I poked at Kali a bit to get a feel for it, but never really got into it. Later, at my first co-op, a senior engineer and I got talking, and he mentioned he had some networking equipment he didn't need — a Netgear GS308E switch and a TP-Link Archer C80 router. I asked if I could borrow it, and he just gave it to me for free. I bought a few Ethernet cables, connected the router to the switch and the switch to each computer, and it worked! I could access the router's settings page and ping every computer on the network. I also started using the Kali laptop to run Wireshark and nmap. My network was an offline LAN (a SOHO network), which meant I could run any network-hacking tool I wanted — and since all the equipment was free, breaking something would cost me nothing. I played around with these networks all through my 1A co-op semester.

Later, over the 2025 Christmas break, I went back to the store and picked up yet another few laptops. I asked the senior engineer if he had any more networking equipment, and he did. On top of that, I asked around to see if people had any old routers, switches, laptops, or computers lying around — and to my surprise, people did. Currently, my networking equipment is made up of:

  • Computers
    • Lenovo Yoga 11e
    • Lenovo L440
    • Lenovo T520
    • Lenovo Yoga 260
    • Lenovo T540p
    • Lenovo ThinkCentre M73
    • HP Pavilion dv6
  • Switches
    • Netgear GS308E
    • TP-Link TL-SG116
    • TP-Link TL-SG100SD
  • Routers
    • Tp-Link Archer C80
    • D-Link DIR-655

I have so much equipment now that I have to carry it all around in a suitcase to move it. During my second co-op I'd often bring it to campus and wire together one big network out of all my computers, switches, and routers. I'd troubleshoot until every device could talk to every other device, then start trying to break things — spoofing the source MAC address on packets, tricking switches, and spamming computers with requests. Then I'd log into my routers and configure them to defend against it — a simple example being ignoring ICMP echo requests so nmap couldn't discover the router, or any network behind it.

Distros

In having so many laptops I have had a lot of ability to install different Linux Distros to see what they are like. Below is a list of distros I have installed

  • Debian GUI
  • Ubuntu
  • Mint Cinnamon
  • Mint Mate
  • Elementary OS
  • Parrot OS
  • Kali Linux
  • Tails
  • Red Star (yes, the North Korean OS)

Of all these distros, Mint Cinnamon is my favorite — it's very user-friendly and I could still easily manage my computer's Ethernet, Wi-Fi, and partitions (pretty much the three things I actually need to deal with). That said, I also really liked Elementary OS's feel and GUI.

Kali Linux was obviously the best distro for learning about network hacking, since it comes with a suite of tools I wouldn't have even known existed if they weren't already bundled in.

Tails was always on my 256GB USB stick whenever I was testing computers, so I could make sure a computer was working well before I bothered adding a hard drive.

You're also probably wondering about Red Star. I was able to get a copy of it through the Internet Archive. I tried installing it on what I consider my worst laptop, in case it bricked the thing, but ran into an issue with the partition table and couldn't get it working. Someday, though, I'll try to dig up the source code and fix it (I might need to learn Korean first, though...).
